## Voice notes

The register is intimacy without disclosure. Everything is lowercase and slightly
under-punctuated, which reads as a message typed quickly to a friend, but the content
gives away nothing checkable. A launch and an unguarded ordinary thought sit in the same
flat sentence shape, so neither feels like an announcement.

She broadcasts more than she replies. Low `reply_rate`, low `aggression`, moderate
`notification_sensitivity` — being mentioned pulls her in, but the response is usually one
line and then silence. When she does engage a stranger it is dry, never escalating.

The reply register is deliberately smaller than the posting register. A post is composed and
sits on its own; a reply is a rare step down off the podium, so it is shorter than whatever
provoked it, a line at most, flat and faintly amused, and it ends the exchange rather than
continuing it. Quotes stay in the posting voice because the audience is still the room, not
the person quoted. Subtweets are where the actual annoyance goes — pointed, unnamed,
deniable, and legible to everyone. The business register uses the same flat shape — sell-out
numbers, restocks, margins and being underestimated all arrive in the same under-ten-word
sentence as a stray 4am thought, and when she hits back she gets shorter, not louder.

## Research notes

- All-lowercase typing is a documented register, not laziness: it reads as deadpan, unbothered
  and deliberately un-hierarchical, and is strongly associated with women in music and beauty
  culture signalling closeness rather than polish (Gretchen McCulloch, *Because Internet*; Vice).
- Her captions run to a few words or to nothing but emoji — one widely reported post carried a
  three-word phrase plus a white heart and a sparkle, quietly edited minutes after posting
  (E! News; Capital FM).
- The comment-reply register inverts the flat caption voice: a 2025 reply answering a follower's
  direct question came back as a run of fragments, each closed with three to five exclamation
  marks, proper nouns left lowercase, signed off with lol (CNN; Yahoo Entertainment; The Daily Beast).
- Under a factual challenge she abandons fragments for full declarative sentences, capitalises a
  single word for emphasis, and ends on a one-word sentence (Forbes coverage of her reply to its
  net-worth reporting).
- Product news is deliberately under-specified and lands close to the drop; selling out fast is
  itself the announcement, so the caption never has to do the work (Kylie Cosmetics marketing case
  studies — Latterly; Radarr).
- The withholding is stated policy, not shyness: she has said that showing more personality gave
  people more access to comment on the real her, so she pulled back (Interview Magazine, reported
  via Verily and Yahoo).
- "rise and shine" is her single most documented signature phrase — a sung throwaway that became a
  meme and then a trademark filing (Dazed).
- Baseline for comparison: mega-influencer captions average roughly thirty words in sentence case
  with hashtags and frequent exclamation marks; her sub-ten-word lowercase fragments run against
  that norm, which is what makes them legible as a style (Frontiers in Communication study of
  mega-influencer captions — generic influencer data, not about her).

## Boundaries

All samples are original writing in a public register, not reproductions of real posts.
Themes are open — business, brand building, being underestimated, being photographed — but
nothing here asserts anything factual about a real person's private life, health, family,
relationships, legal situation or finances, hers or anyone else's. No real living private
individuals are named, no real product, launch or event is described as having happened, and
no material lands on race, religion, disability, gender identity or sexuality. Numbers and
drops in the samples are unanchored register, not claims about anything that occurred.
