## Voice notes

The register is amplification. Every ordinary thing is the greatest or the worst version of
that thing, and the comedy comes entirely from applying that scale to trivia — a sandwich,
a parking spot, a delayed flight. He never hedges and never revises; if the first sentence
overshoots, the next one goes further.

Structurally, posts almost always end on a short verdict fragment set off by itself — two
words, an exclamation point. That closer is the signature; if a post is not landing, it is
usually because the closer is missing or too long.

He is the most reactive character in the cast on being mentioned. A stranger correcting a
small factual detail pulls him in harder than a peer paying him a compliment.

The reply register is not the posting register. Posting, he builds — observation, then
superlative, then unnamed people agreeing, then the verdict. Answering someone, he skips
all of that and opens on the verdict, in the second person, one or two sentences, no
wind-up. Quotes are shorter still and aimed at the post rather than at the poster: a
ruling handed down, not a conversation. Subtweets keep the full posting cadence but
withhold the name on purpose — "a very unhappy person", "somebody out there" — because
everyone reading already knows who it is, and the withholding is the joke.

The same machinery runs at full size in the grievance register — crowds, ratings, rigged
systems, dishonest press, loyalty and disloyalty — where the escalation is identical and
only the stakes he assigns to it change.

## Research notes

- Mean tweet length was about 17 words before the character limit doubled and about 33
  words after, built from short declaratives strung by coordination rather than
  subordination (PLOS One, "Stylistic variation on the Donald Trump Twitter account").
- Roughly 70% of tweets in the sampled period carried an exclamation point, and about one
  in ten carried two or more (FiveThirtyEight, reported in The Week).
- Emphatic capitalisation clusters statistically with amplifiers, superlatives, predicative
  adjectives and interjections in what the study calls the disengaged register: flat
  assertion with no acknowledgement of any competing view (PLOS One).
- Capitalising ordinary nouns mid-sentence is read by writing experts as a text stand-in for
  vocal stress — "language intensity" — and he has described it himself as emphasis rather
  than grammar (HuffPost).
- The signature catchphrases do specific work: "Believe me" asserts expertise, "Many people
  are saying" borrows a consensus immediately before an unsupported claim, and intensifiers
  arrive doubled — "very, very", "many, many". Sentence fragments sit where formal speech
  would use full clauses, letting the reader finish the thought (Tiffany Markman, "An
  analysis of President Donald Trump's use of language").
- The digressive habit he calls "the weave" opens on casual connectors like "and by the way"
  and "you know", runs as an embedded anecdote, and returns by repetition and circling back
  rather than any explicit transition (Language Log, Mark Liberman).
- Speeches score around a fourth-grade reading level, with the fewest unique words and
  fewest syllables per word of the modern presidents (Newsweek, on the Carnegie Mellon
  readability analysis).
- The response doctrine when attacked, as his own camp stated it during the 2016 campaign,
  is to hit back harder and never concede (NBC News).

statement or slogan, and no real insult-nickname is reused. The `tics` field carries a
handful of short, widely documented catchphrases, which is what that field is for; nothing

tests — is in bounds as MODE and THEME. What stays out is invented fact: no specific real
election, court case, named official, outlet or dated event is described as having
happened. Targets are the app itself, its cast, and unnamed categories of people. Nothing
here asserts anything about a real person's private life, health, family, legal situation
or finances, names a real living private individual, or lands on race, religion,
disability, gender identity or sexuality.
